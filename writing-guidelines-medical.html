Context

When words carry clinical weight

Patient Safety Notice

Writing and UX design for medical products can directly impact clinical outcomes. One mistake in the content or design can prove to be fatal. These guidelines ensure every word supports safe and correct use of the product — written as if the content could be used under time pressure, by non-native speakers, in stressful or clinical environments.

Content for medical products must ensure clear information hierarchy, error prevention mechanisms, and intuitive data visualisation to minimise risk in healthcare product design.

Core Principles

What is important

01
Patient safety and clinical accuracy come first
Every word must support safe and correct use of the product. Prefer explicit instructions over implied meaning.
Correct"Press Start to begin the test."
Avoid"You're good to go."
02
Clarity over brevity
Short is good — but only if meaning stays unambiguous. Use one instruction per sentence. Avoid collapsing steps.
Correct"Clean the probe. Then attach it to the device."
Avoid"Prepare the probe."
03
Consistent, standard medical terminology
Use clinically accepted terms, not internal jargon. Use the same term everywhere — UI, help text, IFU. If multiple terms exist, choose one and lock it.
Correct"Tinnitus" (used everywhere)
Avoid"Ringing", "ear noise", "sound issues" (mixed usage)
04
Clear intent and instruction hierarchy
Structure content so users can instantly understand: what is happening, what they need to do, and what happens next.

Error flow example

Error
Microphone not detected
Action

Result

05
Neutral, professional, instructional tone
Write like a medical professional, not a marketer. Calm, factual, respectful. No persuasion, no exaggeration.
Correct"This feature records hearing test results."
Avoid"This powerful feature gives you amazing insights!"
06
Regulatory alignment
Content must align with approved labelling, IFU, intended use, and risk classifications. Do not introduce new claims, benefits, or promises outside approved scope.
Correct"Designed to support hearing assessments."
Avoid"Improves hearing outcomes."
07
Accessibility and health literacy
Assume varied literacy and cognitive load. Prefer common sentence structure, active voice, and concrete verbs. Avoid medical shorthand unless the audience is strictly clinical.
Correct"Remove the hearing aid before testing."
Avoid"Device removal required prior to assessment."
Anti-Patterns

What to avoid

Writing failures that can introduce clinical risk, regulatory non-compliance, or user confusion in medical contexts.

01
Ambiguity
Phrases like "as needed", "if necessary", or "when appropriate" leave critical decisions to chance.
✗ "Adjust volume if needed." ✓ "Adjust the volume until the tone is clearly audible."
02
Idioms, metaphors, and casual language
No idioms, no humour, no conversational filler. These create localization errors and undermine clinical authority.
✗ "Let's get started!" ✓ "Start the setup."
03
Marketing or emotional language
Words like "best", "powerful", "life-changing", "seamless" have no place in medical product copy.
✗ "A revolutionary hearing experience." ✓ "A hearing assessment tool."
04
Implicit medical advice
Do not suggest diagnosis or treatment unless explicitly approved in the product's labelling and regulatory scope.
✗ "This result indicates hearing loss." ✓ "This result may require review by a hearing care professional."
05
Vague error and system messages
Never blame the user. Always explain impact and next step. A vague error in a medical context can cause a patient to take the wrong action.
✗ "Something went wrong." ✓ "The test could not be completed. Restart the device and try again."
06
Overloading one message
Avoid combining status, instruction, and explanation in one sentence. Each element should do one job only.
✗ Compound messages mixing state, instruction and detail. ✓ Separate status, action, and result clearly.
